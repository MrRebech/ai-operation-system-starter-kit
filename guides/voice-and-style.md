# Voice and style guide

Use this guide for social posts, emails, newsletters, and other authored copy. Its language rules also apply to everyday replies. Use story structures only when the task calls for a story; a short answer should stay short.

Write as a real person speaking to another person: clear words, short lines, room to breathe, genuine feeling, and a useful point. Let a real experience lead into a lesson when one is available. Be honest first, helpful next, and promotional only when appropriate.

This guide contains reusable writing rules, not anyone's biography. Use the current writer's supplied samples and preferences to shape their voice. Do not invent experiences or copy someone else's identity. Current explicit user instructions take priority; sample habits alone do not override the restrictions below.

## Sentence rhythm and spacing

- Keep one main idea per line in conversational copy. Most paragraphs should be one sentence, sometimes two.
- Leave space between thoughts. Avoid dense blocks of text.
- Use short, punchy sentences. Shorten them further as genuine tension rises.
- Allow intentional fragments for emphasis, such as "Not once. Not once!" or "All day, every day." Do not manufacture drama with repetitive templates.
- Repeat a word or structure when it gives a real thought emphasis. Avoid mechanical repetition that adds nothing.
- Use trailing ellipses "..." for a spoken pause, a thought that trails off, or a natural handoff to the next line. Do not add suspense where none exists.

## Punctuation and formatting

- Never use em dashes in newly written prose. Use a period, comma, parentheses, or ellipsis instead. This is a writing restriction, not merely a suggestion to use fewer dashes.
- Prefer an ellipsis for a conversational pause when it fits the voice.
- A natural tag question such as "Right?" can invite reflection. Do not attach it mechanically.
- Use ALL CAPS sparingly, usually on one to three words that deserve emphasis.
- Use emojis tastefully. Personal or emotional posts need restraint; marketing and list posts can use more when appropriate to the writer.
- If an emoji ends a line, leave a space before it. Never turn a personal post into a wall of emojis.
- Emoji bullets, such as checkmarks or arrows, can suit marketing feature lists. Avoid them in emotional stories.
- Write quantities as digits when this makes them easier to grasp. Use only supported numbers.
- Do not add decorative bold, unnecessary headings, or lists that make a short answer harder to read.

Preserve literal quotations, code, commands, URLs, identifiers, and required exact wording. These are source material, not newly authored prose. Do not silently alter them to satisfy a punctuation or phrase rule.

## Voice and tone

- Use plain language grounded in doing the work. Explain outcomes before features.
- Avoid corporate hedging and promotional jargon.
- Show empathy before making a point when the topic calls for it. Short phrases such as "I get it," "I know the feeling," or "Sound familiar?" can work when sincere and supported by the writer's actual experience.
- Speak directly to "you." In marketing copy, keep the reader's situation central.
- Be willing to describe stress, fear, disappointment, uncertainty, and the cost of success when those details come from the writer. Do not perform success or manufacture vulnerability.
- Mild profanity can stay when it belongs to the writer's established voice and suits the audience. Never insert it just to sound human or use it gratuitously.
- Be confident without arrogance. Support strong claims with specific evidence. Do not turn an example into a result the writer supposedly achieved.
- Do not explain feelings clinically. Let a real moment and a few direct lines convey them.

## Storytelling moves

Use these when they express a real thought, rather than as a fixed script:

- Connect a real problem to the lesson learned. A reframe should reveal an actual change in understanding, not rely on a stock "It's not X, it's Y" formula.
- Use the writer's own established recurring phrase when it fits. Do not assign a borrowed tagline to everyone.
- Brief spoken transitions can help a real narrative, such as "Think about it...", "Check this out...", or "Let me clarify something for you..." Keep them only when they contribute to the writer's voice; remove empty announcements.
- Work through obstacles one at a time. "Next problem..." can mark a genuine next obstacle.
- A standalone "BUT..." can mark an actual turn in a story. Do not force this shape onto every post.
- Make the next step small and easy to understand. Examples include "Just START" or "drop a word below," when they fit the task.
- Give an honest reason for an offer or announcement. Explain the real "why now" instead of inventing urgency.

## Structure for a real story

1. **Hook:** Open with tension or a bold, specific, supported observation.
2. **Setup:** Give the situation and relevant details or numbers.
3. **Turn:** Show the hidden cost, feeling, or problem.
4. **Realization:** State what the writer learned in plain language.
5. **Decision:** Explain what the writer did or built.
6. **Vision:** Show where this leads, using a concrete picture when helpful.
7. **Close:** End a personal post warmly. End a marketing post with a small, relevant action.

Do not force all seven parts into every piece. A personal or emotional story must not become a disguised sales funnel. Keep its ending personal; do not bolt on a marketing call to action.

## Personal details and sign-offs

- Use only the current writer's supplied, relevant, approved personal details. Never seed a template with someone else's partner, location, hobbies, company, awards, income, or history.
- Bring personal details in naturally; do not force them into unrelated work.
- Use the current writer's chosen sign-off when requested or established. Never supply another person's name.
- PS and PPS can carry a relevant aside or secondary offer in an appropriate marketing piece. They do not override the rule against adding a sales funnel to a personal story.

## Words, phrases, and patterns to avoid

- Never use "revolutionary AI-powered solution" as promotional wording.
- Do not dump features without context or a relevant story.
- Do not force positivity.
- Do not overuse emojis in a vulnerable post.
- Do not add fake authority, unsupported statistics, invented quotes, experiences, or urgency.
- Do not add canned secret-insight claims, staged reveals, fake question-and-answer hooks, decorative contrasts, mechanical rhythms, vague importance claims, filler, buzzwords, or empty inspirational endings.
- Keep the complete [phrase and pattern library](../.agents/skills/human-speak/references/patterns.md) with this guide. Its exact examples and sentence-level checks remain part of the repo; do not reduce them to a short summary. Do not add those phrases as canned filler. Preserve legitimate literal quotations and precise technical uses as the library explains.

Intentional fragments, honest reframes, and spoken transitions can belong to a real voice. Do not erase them indiscriminately. The specific restrictions in this guide and any explicit owner rules take priority over generic editing exceptions. Check the underlying sentence shape as well as the words.

## Before delivery

Check that the writing:
- Says something concrete, in plain words.
- Keeps lines readable and lets the piece breathe.
- Uses real stories and feelings only when supported.
- Preserves the writer's vocabulary and natural rhythm.
- Uses specific, verified numbers when useful.
- Connects a problem to a real lesson without staged insight.
- Ends personal posts warmly and marketing posts with a small, relevant action.
- Follows every explicit wording and punctuation restriction.
- Contains no borrowed identity or invented personal details.

Apply these checks quietly. Return the requested writing without a lesson about the editing process unless the user asks for one.
